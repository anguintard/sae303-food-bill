Northern Ireland’s food bill has risen twice as fast as England’s and the basket is no healthier

Brexit, Covid and the war in Ukraine each pushed the weekly shop up. Households here now pay a fifth more than before the crises and are not buying more food for it.

By Guintard Antoine November 25, 2026 · 5 min read

Households in Northern Ireland now spend more on food at home than in any other UK nation. In 2024-25 the weekly shop came to £41.28 per person, against £37.23 in England, according to the latest Family Food figures from the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra).

The gap has opened over a decade of shocks. Comparing the three years before the pandemic with the three most recent ones, spending on food and drink at home rose by 21% here. That is more than twice the 10% recorded in England, and ahead of Scotland (18%) and Wales (4%).

Key points

  • Spending on food at home rose by 21% in Northern Ireland, against 10% in England
  • Unlike England’s, the bill here never fell back to its pre-Covid level
  • Households here buy less fruit and vegetables than in England, and more bread and processed meat

Yet the extra money has not bought more food. Households are taking home less milk, bread and fresh fruit than before the pandemic, and they still buy fewer vegetables and less fruit than their neighbours across the Irish Sea.

The index sets each nation’s spending in 2019-20, the last year before Covid, at 100. Northern Ireland’s line jumps to 115 in the first year of the pandemic, dips, then climbs to a peak of 122 in 2023-24. In 2024-25 it stands at 120, against 114 in England.

Lockdowns explain part of that first jump: with cafés and restaurants shut, more meals were eaten at home across the UK. But England’s bill fell back below its pre-Covid level in 2021-22.

England’s bill briefly fell back below its pre-Covid level. Here, it never did.

The figures alone cannot say how much of the gap comes from the Protocol, the cost of moving goods across the Irish Sea or other local factors. What they do show is that each shock has left Northern Ireland with a higher bill than before.

Paying more, buying less

In Northern Ireland, the price paid for everyday staples has climbed while the amount bought has fallen. On a chart, the two lines open like scissors.

Take milk. In 2019-20 households here paid an average of £1.03 a litre; by 2024-25 that had risen to £1.46, an increase of 42%. Over the same period, the amount bought fell from 1,884 ml to 1,556 ml per person each week.

Bread tells the same story: up 32% to £3.16 a kilo, while the amount bought fell by a fifth. Fresh fruit rose by 24% to £3.14 a kilo. Use the buttons above the chart to compare the three.

Smaller baskets are not unique to Northern Ireland: the amount of milk and bread bought has fallen across the UK. The difference is the size of the bill households here are left with.

What’s in the basket

What goes into the basket matters as much as what it costs. Compared with England, households here buy 24% fewer vegetables and 17% less fresh fruit, but 20% more bread and 17% more processed meat.

Each week, that adds up to 192 g less fruit and vegetables per person than in England, and 211 g more bread and processed meat. Fish is lower too, at 112 g against 127 g.

A decade that has left its mark

Ten years after a Brexit vote that most people here rejected, the figures describe a region paying more for its food than any other UK nation, without eating better for it.

With prices still high, the weekly shop remains one of the clearest measures of how the crises of the past decade have been felt in Northern Ireland.

About the data

Figures come from Defra’s Family Food datasets (FYE 2025 update), based on the Living Costs and Food Survey. They cover food and drink bought for the home, per person per week, in financial years running from April to March. Spending is in cash terms and is not adjusted for inflation.

Because the Northern Ireland sample is small, headline comparisons use three-year averages (2017-20 against 2022-25) rather than single years. The average price is spending divided by the amount bought, so it also moves when households switch products.

The 2019-20 eating-out figures for Northern Ireland and Scotland are identical in the source file, so we left them out of our calculations. The data does not include household incomes by region.
